THE TALE OF JACK AND THE BEANSTALK

Chapter 1: The Market Trade

Once upon a time, at the edge of a quiet green valley, there lived a poor widow and her only son, Jack. They owned nothing in the world except a small wooden cottage, a patch of rocky garden soil, and a gentle white cow named Milky-White. Every morning, Milky-White gave fresh milk that Jack carried down the winding dirt road to sell in the village market, and for many years that was just enough to buy bread and firewood.

One cold autumn morning, however, Milky-White stopped giving milk altogether. Jack's mother sat by the empty hearth, wrapping her shawl tightly around her shoulders. Without milk to sell, they had no coins left for flour, oats, or winter coal. With a heavy heart, she told Jack that he must lead Milky-White to the market town and sell her for the best price he could find.

Jack promised he would make a wise bargain. He tied a braided rope around Milky-White's neck and set off along the lane just as the sun rose over the eastern hills. Before he had walked halfway to town, he met a small, brisk old man wearing a patched green coat and a wide-brimmed hat.

"Good morning, Jack," said the old man, tipping his hat with a twinkle in his eyes. "Where are you taking that fine cow so early in the day?"

"Good morning to you, sir," Jack replied politely. "I am taking Milky-White to the market to sell her, for we have no money left at home."

"You look like a clever lad," the old man said, reaching into his coat pocket. "Save yourself the long walk to town. I will trade you these five magic beans for your cow right here and now."

Jack stared at the beans resting in the old man's wrinkled palm. They were unlike any beans he had ever seen: one was deep sapphire blue, one gleamed like polished copper, one was speckled silver, one shone emerald green, and the fifth looked like a tiny drop of captured starlight.

"Magic beans?" Jack asked doubtfully. "My mother needs silver coins to buy bread."

"Ah, but ordinary silver is spent in a week," the old man whispered. "Plant these five beans in your garden tonight, and by sunrise tomorrow they will grow right up into the clouds, bringing fortune beyond anything the market could offer. If they do not sprout by morning, you may come find me at the crossroads and take your cow back."

Drawn by the strange shimmer of the beans and the sincerity in the old man's voice, Jack agreed. He handed over Milky-White's rope, tucked the five colorful beans safely into his pocket, and hurried home eager to share the news.

Chapter 2: The Giant Stalk

When Jack arrived home before noon, his mother was delighted to see him back so soon, thinking he must have sold Milky-White for a bag of gold. When Jack proudly opened his hand and showed her the five shiny beans, however, her smile vanished.

"Have you lost your wits, Jack?" she cried in dismay. "We needed coins for supper, and you traded our only cow for a handful of garden beans!"

Too upset to eat, she tossed the five beans straight out the open kitchen window into the dark soil below, and sent Jack upstairs to his attic room without supper. Jack lay awake for a long time listening to the wind rustle through the trees, wondering if he had made a terrible mistake.

When the first pale light of dawn crept across his wooden floorboards, Jack noticed something unusual. Instead of bright morning sunshine streaming through his small window, the room was filled with a deep, leafy green shadow.

Jack jumped out of bed and threw open the shutters. There, rising directly from the garden beneath his window, was a colossal beanstalk thicker than a hundred oak trees twisted together. Its sturdy green vines braided upward like a natural staircase, twisting higher and higher until the top vanished completely into a sea of white morning clouds.

The old man had told the truth after all. Without waking his mother, Jack pulled on his boots, climbed out onto the nearest thick leaf, and began to ascend.

Up and up he climbed, hand over hand, foot over foot. Soon the cottage looked no larger than a pebble, and the river winding through the valley looked like a thin silver ribbon. Still Jack climbed, steady and determined, until at last he broke through the cool, misty layer of clouds and stepped out onto a broad, flat road made of smooth white stone.

Chapter 3: The Castle Above the Clouds

Jack followed the white stone road across the sky until he reached an enormous castle with towers that scraped the upper heavens. Everything was built on a titanic scale: the front door was as tall as a church steeple, and each stone step was as high as Jack's chest.

Standing on the threshold was a towering woman with a kind, tired face and a large white apron. Jack took off his cap and bowed low.

"Good morning, ma'am," Jack called up as loudly as he could. "Could you spare a crust of bread and a cup of water for a hungry traveler?"

The giant woman looked down in surprise, then shook her head warningly. "Bless my soul, boy, what are you doing up here? My husband is a fierce ogre who eats travelers for breakfast! You must run away before he returns from his morning walk."

"Please, ma'am," Jack pleaded, "I have climbed since dawn and have not eaten since yesterday afternoon."

The giantess felt sorry for the polite boy. She scooped him up gently, carried him into the vast kitchen, and set him beside a massive iron stove. There she gave him a chunk of warm bread as big as a pillow and a bowl of sweet milk the size of a washbasin.

Jack had barely finished half his meal when the entire castle began to tremble. Thump! Thump! Thump! Heavy footsteps echoed down the stone corridor, rattling the copper pots hanging from the rafters.

"Quickly, into the empty oven with you!" whispered the giantess, opening a heavy iron door beside the hearth and pushing Jack inside just as her husband strode into the room.

Chapter 4: The Bags of Gold

The giant was enormous, with a beard like tangled storm clouds and a voice that sounded like rolling thunder. He sniffed the air suspiciously and roared his famous rhyme:

"Fee-fi-fo-fum! I smell the blood of an Englishman! Be he alive, or be he dead, I'll grind his bones to make my bread!"

"Nonsense, dear," said his wife briskly, setting a huge platter of roasted oxen and baked potatoes on the oak table. "You only smell the sheep you brought home yesterday. Sit down and eat your breakfast before it grows cold."

Grumbling under his breath, the giant sat down and ate an entire feast. Afterward, he called for his counting chest. His wife hauled out two heavy leather sacks brimming with gleaming gold coins and placed them on the table.

For an hour, Jack peeked through a narrow crack in the oven door while the giant counted stack after stack of bright gold pieces. At last, exhausted from his meal, the giant rested his chin on his massive arms and fell fast asleep, snoring so loudly that the windowpanes shook.

Jack saw his chance. Quiet as a mouse, he slipped out of the iron oven, crept across the stone flagstones, and lifted one of the heavy leather bags of gold. With all his strength, he dragged it out the kitchen door, down the white stone road, and safely down the twisting ladders of the giant beanstalk.

When Jack reached the cottage garden and poured the shining gold coins onto the kitchen table, his mother wept with joy. With that gold, they bought back Milky-White, repaired the leaking roof, and lived comfortably all through the winter.

Chapter 5: The Hen That Laid Golden Eggs

By the following spring, the gold coins were nearly gone, and Jack decided to climb the beanstalk once more. He rose early, scaled the thick green vines into the clouds, and walked back to the great stone castle.

Once again, the giantess stood at the doorway, and though she hesitated at first, Jack spoke so politely that she let him inside for breakfast. Just as before, thunderous footsteps shook the floor, and Jack hid inside the copper kettle on the shelf.

After eating, the giant called out, "Wife, bring me my little brown hen!"

She placed a plump brown hen on the table. Whenever the giant commanded, "Lay!" the hen fluttered her wings and laid a solid, gleaming egg of pure gold. Before long, the giant nodded off to sleep, his head resting on his crossed arms.

Jack crept out from the kettle, tucked the golden hen carefully under his arm so she would not cluck, and raced back to the beanstalk. He climbed down swiftly and presented the wonderful hen to his mother. From that day forward, whenever they needed money for food or clothes, the hen laid a golden egg, and they never knew poverty again.

Chapter 6: The Singing Harp

Still, curiosity tugged at Jack's heart. One bright summer morning, he climbed the beanstalk a third and final time. Knowing the giantess might recognize him, he slipped quietly through a side window into the great hall and hid inside a tall wooden laundry chest.

Soon the giant marched in, ate his dinner, and demanded his golden harp. His wife set a small harp carved from shining gold upon the table. When the giant said, "Sing!" the harp played the sweetest, most enchanting music Jack had ever heard, lulling the giant into a deep slumber within minutes.

Jack lifted the lid of the chest, tiptoed to the table, and grasped the golden harp. The moment he lifted it, however, the harp cried out clearly, "Master! Master!"

The giant's eyes snapped open. Seeing Jack running toward the doorway with his prized harp, the giant leaped up with a roar that shook the rafters and charged after him.

Jack sprinted down the white stone road with the singing harp clutched to his chest. He reached the edge of the clouds and scrambled down the beanstalk faster than he had ever climbed in his life, while the entire stalk swayed wildly under the giant's enormous weight right above him.

"Mother! Bring the axe!" Jack shouted as he neared the garden.

His mother ran out with the woodcutter's sharp axe. The instant Jack's boots touched the grass, he seized the axe and swung with all his might. One blow, two blows, three—the mighty beanstalk split apart and crashed harmlessly into the empty fields beyond the valley, bringing the giant's reign to an end forever.

Jack and his mother lived happily and peacefully in their cottage for the rest of their days, sharing their good fortune with every neighbor in the valley.
